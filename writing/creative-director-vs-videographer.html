Creative director vs videographer: what actually changes

A videographer decides inside the frame. A creative director decides before there is one.

By Emaan Khan, who shot and cut his own work for four years before the job became deciding what other people shoot. More about me.

What is the difference between a creative director and a videographer?

A videographer is responsible for the footage. A creative director is responsible for the decision the footage carries out, which means most of the job is finished before anybody picks up a camera.

Adobe’s comparison guide describes videographers as typically a one-person team or a small crew whose job is to capture footage, and quotes the cinematographer Josh Miller saying something I would put on a wall: “Directing is actually one of the biggest things as a videographer that you need to learn.” The entry for the other title goes the other way and defines a creative director as the person who makes high-level creative decisions, adding, in the fashion section, that the creative director “does not design clothes.”

Both jobs are creative. They spend the creativity at different hours of the week.

The bottom row is the whole difference, and nobody tells you that the measure changes.

What does a creative director actually do all day?

Most of it is writing things down before other people act on them, and then reading the work back against what was written. Shooting is a small and late part of the week.

The arc comes before the script. Before a line is written, the shape goes up: what the first beat makes you need, what the second one answers, where the one turn sits. If the arc does not hold on its own, the script cannot rescue it, and writing the script first is how you hide that from yourself for two days.

Then the card. Every video leaves my hands as one document: the voiceover in a list, camera and mood direction in grey underneath it, and under every line of dialogue, in capitals, how it is meant to be played. [NERVOUS.] [FLAT, LIKE IT IS OBVIOUS.] That bracket is the smallest thing on the page and it decides more takes than the writing does, because a line survives a bad reading and a tone does not.

Then it goes to one person by name. Not to a channel, and not to whoever is free. The person who gets it is the one whose instincts the piece needs, and that routing is a decision I make without thinking about it and would struggle to explain, which is a sign I should write it down.

Then the notes. A note that says cut the first two seconds fixes one video. A note that says cut the first two seconds, the hook does not start until he moves, gives the editor a rule they keep. I only learned to write the second kind after giving the same note enough times to notice I was giving it again.

That is the day. Briefs, routing, notes, and an argument with myself about whether the idea is any good.

What you give up

You stop being the person who makes it good, and that is a worse feeling than it sounds like.

For the first stretch I kept re-cutting things myself. It was faster, the result was better, and it taught nobody anything. The version of this job where I am the best editor in the room is the version where the work can never be better than one person’s Tuesday. Handing a cut back with three sentences on it is slower on the day and compounds for a year.

The other thing you give up is being able to point at what you did. A shot is yours. A brief that worked is invisible, and the evidence that you did the job well is that the finished thing looks like it was always going to turn out that way.

Questions people actually ask

Are a director and a videographer the same thing?

No, though on a small job one person does both, which is where the confusion comes from. If you shoot your own ideas you are doing both jobs and being paid for one of them, and that is the thing to fix before the title is.

What position is higher than creative director?

In an agency, usually an executive creative director or a chief creative officer. If you work for yourself the ladder does not apply, and the only promotion available is a wider brief: more of the decision, earlier.

What does a creative director do day to day?

Writes the brief, decides who makes it, reads the work back against the brief, and holds the standard. The camera is somebody else’s instrument by then.
